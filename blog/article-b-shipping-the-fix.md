# The fix that had to prove itself six times

The [previous piece](falsification-and-diagnosis.md) ended with a diagnosis: two questions my RAG pipeline kept getting wrong — an IDLH (immediately dangerous to life or health) comparison and an acetone flash point — turned out to be one defect. The chunker had merged multiple records into single oversized chunks wherever the source was tabular, and every fact inside was diluted below the reach of dense retrieval. Two hard targets, one root cause: fat, multi-record chunks.

This piece is what happened when I fixed it. But it isn't really a fix story — it's the same story told six times, climbing one layer of the stack at each telling. The re-chunking worked, and then I had to *prove* it worked, and proving it meant answering a harder version of the same question at every level: **did the chunk get retrieved? did the answer state the fact? did the metric clear the bar for the right reason? did the aggregate mean anything? did the fix I was about to build address the problem I actually had? did the thing I deployed actually contain the code?** At each of those six layers — chunk, answer, threshold, aggregate, hypothesis, deployment — I had an instrument ready to hand me a confident verdict, and at each one the instrument was wrong (or, at the first layer, merely insufficient) in a way only the raw artifact could catch. That climb is the article — and it genuinely starts in the previous piece, where that same rank probe over-reported at the chunk layer. The lesson from there — *the metric triages; the artifact arbitrates* — doesn't get restated here. It gets demonstrated, layer after layer, each one higher up.

## The lever the defect implied

A defect with a shape tells you the shape of the lever. If the problem is that five chemicals share one vector, the fix is to give each record its own. That's **structure-aware re-chunking**: instead of splitting a document on blind character counts, split it on its natural *record boundary* — for the NIOSH Pocket Guide, one chunk per chemical entry; for a safety data sheet, one chunk per section. Each fact gets a vector that means that fact, and only that fact.

The trap in a change like this is that a boundary rule you tuned on your two target documents quietly misfires everywhere else. So before re-chunking anything, I validated the transformation at **corpus scale** — the discipline that de-risks a structural change. The NIOSH delimiter keys on each entry's name line; run naively it orphaned the names of 25 of 677 entries where the PDF's line breaks were inconsistent, so I anchored it more carefully and re-checked across the *whole* guide: **0 of 677 orphaned.** The SDS section rule was worse-behaved. The obvious `SECTION N:` regex matched the Sigma-Aldrich acetone sheet perfectly — **16 of 16 sections** — but tested across the other three vendors it fell apart: Airgas **1 of 16**, Nutrien **0 of 16**, Fisher **0 of 16**, because each vendor formats its section headers differently. A single regex could not safely section all four. So I narrowed the scope to acetone only, where the delimiter provably works, rather than shipping a rule that would silently mangle three other documents. The corpus-scale check turned a plausible one-size rule into a scoped one that I could prove wouldn't collateral-damage the rest of the corpus.

## A control group you don't have to trust

Re-chunking isn't a query-time knob; it re-embeds documents into the vector store. That makes it dangerous in a way the earlier retrieval-depth change wasn't: *every* row's retrieval could shift, including the 26 rows that had nothing to do with the two bugs. How do you know you didn't break something unrelated?

The usual answer is "run the eval and check." The better answer is to make the question unnecessary. I re-chunked into a brand-new **namespace** — a separate labelled partition of the vector store, so I could build the new index alongside the live one and switch between them — using what I'll call the copy-and-replace build: take the 17 untouched documents and copy their existing vectors **byte-identical** into the new namespace, and re-chunk only the 2 target documents. The result was **886 vectors copied + 870 re-chunked = 1,756**. The point of copying rather than re-embedding the untouched docs is that it makes "did I disturb the 17 innocent documents?" answerable **by construction**: their vectors are the same bytes they always were, so there is nothing to disturb. A two-sided reconcile plus a verify-by-refetch confirmed **0 copy bugs**.

One honest asterisk, because "by construction" applies to the copied vectors and not to every downstream retrieval. The 17 documents' vectors are identical; what a given *query* retrieves can still move, because a newly-fine NIOSH entry is now its own competitive vector and can occasionally out-rank into a neighboring question's top 10. So retrieval invariance is **verified, not guaranteed**: of the 19 invariant rows, **16 were byte-identical in what they retrieved**, and 3 saw a benign finer-NIOSH intrusion (rows 7, 20, 25) — none of which regressed, and one of which (row 20, a chlorine ceiling-limit question) actually *improved*. The construction guarantees the control group's inputs; reading the 3 movers confirms the guarantee held where it couldn't be constructed.

## Layer one: the chunk

The lowest layer is retrieval: did the fix put the right chunk in front of the model at all? The climb actually starts one piece back, where a rank probe *over-reported* — its tolerant matcher called `FULL_LEVER` on the wrong chemical, and only reading the chunk caught it. Here the instrument is milder. Before re-chunking, the ammonia IDLH entry was absent from the top 100 for its query. After: **rank 8** for the full comparison question, **rank 2** for the focused sub-question. The chunk was findable again.

But a rank is not an answer, and this is where an instrument that isn't *wrong* can still be *insufficient*. Rank 8 means the chunk is now in the context window. It does *not* mean the model used it, or used both halves of a two-source comparison — being retrievable is not being used. That gap is the next layer.

## Layer two: the answer

The only way to know the model used what retrieval surfaced is to read what the pipeline actually generated — which stated **"NIOSH IDLH … 300 ppm [niosh-pocket-guide page=45] … EPA RMP … 200 ppm"** and then made the comparison the question asked for. That read is the win. The correctness score for the row went **0.363 → 0.974**, and it's nice to have, but I want to be precise about what carries the weight here: the number moved because the *answer* now contains both values and the comparison, and I know that because I read the answer, not because I trusted the delta. (The citation is a small bonus — it self-corrected from page 44 to page 45, landing exactly on the ground-truth page, which a pre-registered prediction had said finer chunks would do.)

## Layer three: the threshold that failed for being too good

Layer three is the promotion gate — the threshold that decides whether this namespace ships. I'd pre-registered it before running anything, which is the whole point of a gate: **promote only if all five metrics land within ±0.03 of the shipped baseline**, plus the IDLH recovery is read-verified and no passing row flips to a confident-wrong answer.

The run came back and the gate failed. Not because anything regressed — because two metrics *improved past the band*: answer relevancy **+0.0351** and context recall **+0.0458**, both above +0.03, both in the right direction. My own instrument was telling me not to promote a namespace because it had gotten too good.

That's not a close call to argue on the merits; it's a bug in the gate. A **symmetric** ±0.03 band is coherent for detecting drift but incoherent as a *promotion* rule, because it treats improvement and regression as equally disqualifying — you'd block a ship for over-performing. So I fixed the spec, in the open: the corrected clause is **asymmetric — no metric regresses beyond −0.03, and improvements are unbounded.** I want to own that plainly rather than dress it up: I mis-specified my own pre-registered bar, and I only noticed because it failed in a direction that embarrassed it. What keeps the fix from being motivated reasoning — rewriting the rules until you pass — is that it was **forced by two improvements**. Had relevancy and recall moved *down* instead of up, the correction would have made no difference to the outcome; it cannot have been reverse-engineered toward a promotion I wanted. That's the test for fixing an instrument mid-project: change it in the open, and make sure the change couldn't have been steered by the result you were hoping for. And the change is standing, not one-off: every promotion bar is written asymmetrically from the outset now — the fix was to how the gates get specified, not just to this one gate. Under the corrected bar the namespace was promotion-eligible, and it was promoted — it's the default namespace every `/ask` request now uses. *(There's a small artifact of the process in the record itself: the like-for-like eval doc concluded "not promoted in this doc" while the shipped default said otherwise — the doc measured its own run, the promotion was a separate later call. That gap was later found and reconciled, and the [metrics ledger](../eval/METRICS_HISTORY.md) now records the promotion against the corrected bar. The doc and the shipped state disagreed; reading both is what caught it.)*

## Layer four: the aggregate that drifted

Layer four is the aggregate itself — and here the instrument lied by a mechanism that has nothing to do with my pipeline at all. The first gated run's aggregates looked mildly alarming: faithfulness down **0.024**, context precision down **0.036**. Read at face value, the fix had cost me two metrics.

It hadn't. That run had been scored under one OpenAI backend `system_fingerprint` (`fp_c881474fd1`) — OpenAI's identifier for the backend configuration serving each call, which drifts when they change the infrastructure underneath you — while the baseline it was being compared against had been generated under a *different* one (`fp_6cc92eaef9`). The models behind those fingerprints are not byte-identical, so half of what looked like "my change moved the metric" was really "I'm comparing across two different generation backends." Fingerprint drift is a confound, and if you don't control for it, your aggregate is measuring the weather.

The fix was a like-for-like: run both namespaces interleaved, question by question, so each row's old-and-new pair is generated back-to-back under the same fingerprint, and only compare rows that genuinely matched. **28 of 28 rows shared `fp_c881474fd1`** — a true matched comparison — and the dips evaporated. Faithfulness went from that scary **−0.024 to +0.008** (over the 19 rows both runs scored without a judge timeout); precision from **−0.036 to −0.019**, comfortably in-band. Nothing had regressed; the drift had been doing the talking.

And the clincher for why you read the text instead of the aggregate is something the project had already caught the judge doing: on the ammonia boiling-point row, two runs with a **byte-identical answer** were scored **0.91 and 0.66** by the correctness judge — the LLM RAGAS uses to score correctness, itself a model and so not deterministic. Same words, quarter-point swing. When the model's output is literally the same string and the *number* still moves, the number is noise — and a small aggregate wobble on a matched fingerprint is exactly that noise summed up. The aggregate tells you where to look; the per-row read of an identical answer tells you the aggregate is lying.

## Layer five: the hypothesis (and the scaffolding I didn't need)

Here's an irony I have to own before the last recovery. Back when I thought the IDLH bug was a decomposition problem, I'd started building a whole LangGraph agent to host that decomposition — a router, a graph, the works. The previous piece is the story of the free probe that killed decomposition before I finished. So by the time re-chunking landed, I had a half-built agent skeleton sitting idle, a solution in search of a problem.

It found one, at layer five: the **hypothesis** layer. Re-chunking recovered IDLH cleanly, but acetone's flash point had improved from rank 107 only to **rank 19** — real movement, but still outside the top 10, and a chunk at rank 19 is as absent from a k=10 answer as one at rank 107. (Same shape as the EPA sub-question a piece ago: a rank that got better without becoming a win.) My first guess was reasonable and specific: per-section chunking still bundles ~40 physical properties into acetone's Section 9, so the flash point is *still* diluted — the fix is finer, per-property chunking. I was ready to build it.

Then I read the 18 chunks that outranked acetone's flash-point chunk, and the guess was wrong. They weren't other acetone properties drowning it out. They were **17 finer NIOSH per-entry chunks** (plus acetone's own Section 1) — the very chunks my IDLH fix had just created — flooding the results for the word "acetone," because the Pocket Guide has an acetone entry and several acetates, all now crisp competitive vectors. The problem had changed underneath my hypothesis: it wasn't dilution anymore, it was *interference*, and the interference was collateral from the fix that solved the other bug. The rank list corrected a plausible hypothesis the same way the answer text corrects a plausible metric — by being the thing you actually read.

The right lever, then, wasn't per-property chunking. When a question names a single document — "the flash point of acetone *per the Sigma-Aldrich SDS*" — you don't need a cleverer global ranking; you need to retrieve *within that one document*. That's a source-scoped router, and it's exactly the payload the idle skeleton was built to carry. The critical distinction, and the reason this isn't the hybrid-retrieval idea an earlier phase had killed: a per-query source filter fires only on single-document questions and can only ever *narrow*, so it cannot demote a passing row in some other document. **4 rows scoped, 24 untouched, 0 evictions** — same idea family as the globally-killed fusion, opposite blast radius. Acetone's correctness went **0.036 → 0.717**.

## Layer six: the deploy that passed every test and shipped nothing

The top of the stack is deployment, and it is where the most dangerous instrument of all told me everything was fine.

I shipped the router behind a new endpoint, `POST /ask/agent`. The test suite was green — every unit and integration test passing. CI was green. I merged, and the platform auto-deployed. By every signal I had, the recovery was live.

Then I ran the post-merge wire-smoke — an actual HTTP request to the actual running service, not the code the tests run against — and `/ask/agent` returned **404**, over and over, for ten minutes of polling. The cause was one missing line in the Dockerfile: it copied `src/` and `api/` into the image but not the new `agent/` package. So the new image's entry point imported a package that wasn't there and crashed on startup — and the platform did the sensible thing and kept the last-healthy *old* image running in its place. That old image had never heard of `/ask/agent`, so it answered the new route with a 404: the error wasn't the new endpoint failing, it was the previous build still serving because the new one never came up. Every test had passed because the tests run against the repository, where `agent/` exists. CI had even built the image. But **nothing had exercised the running service**, and a missing `COPY` only fails at container startup — precisely the moment no test was looking.

This is the broadest lesson in the whole series, and it's worth stating flatly: **CI validates your code. It does not validate that the artifact you deployed contains your code.** Those are different claims, and the gap between them is invisible to every green check mark. The only thing that closes it is exercising the deployed artifact itself — the wire-smoke that hit the live URL and got a 404 no test could have produced.

The fix (a second PR) added the missing `COPY agent/` and, while I was there, made the agent import lazy so a future problem in that package can only ever break `/ask/agent` and never take the core `/ask` down with it. Then the redeploy, and the two wire-smokes that are the actual proof the recovery shipped — the real responses off the live service:

```
POST /ask/agent  — "flash point of acetone per the Sigma-Aldrich SDS?"
{
  "answer": "The flash point of acetone, according to the Sigma-Aldrich Safety Data Sheet,
             is -17.0 °C (measured by closed cup method) [source_doc_id=sds-sigma-aldrich-acetone page=7].",
  "route": "source_scoped",
  "source_doc_id": "sds-sigma-aldrich-acetone",
  "routing_reason": "Question attributed to a single named document: Sigma-Aldrich Safety Data Sheet — Acetone"
}
```

```
POST /ask  — "how does the NIOSH IDLH compare to the EPA RMP toxic endpoint?"
{
  "answer": "The NIOSH IDLH (Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health) concentration for
             anhydrous ammonia is 300 ppm (0.21 mg/m³) [source_doc_id=niosh-pocket-guide page=45].
             In contrast, the EPA RMP (Risk Management Program) toxic endpoint for ammonia is
             200 ppm (0.14 mg/L) [source_doc_id=epa-rmp-ammonia-refrigeration page=1]. Thus, the
             NIOSH IDLH value is higher than the EPA RMP toxic endpoint. Specifically, the IDLH
             is 300 ppm, while the toxic endpoint is 200 ppm."
}
```

## The honest ledger

Both targets are recovered and both are served — but in two different deployment states, and I'd rather say so than blur them. IDLH is served on the default `/ask` endpoint, off the promoted `semantic_v2` namespace, on every request. Acetone is served on the routed `/ask/agent` endpoint, which any request can opt into but which isn't the default. Two wins, two different places on the wire.

And one limitation stays on the books rather than getting quietly buried: the acetone answer above cites **page 7** for a value whose ground truth is page 8. The document is right and the value is right; the page is off by one. It's the same **provenance-versus-fact-location** split the build write-up names: the structured citation list is provenance — the pages of the *retrieved* chunks, correct by construction — while the exact page of the fact isn't guaranteed, because a chunk labeled by its start page can hold a value a page or two further in. Page-level precision is an open concern, recorded here because a ledger you can trust is one that lists what still doesn't work.

One more entry belongs in an honest ledger: of the six instruments that lied in this piece, which can lie again? **Two are now caught by construction rather than by memory.** Every result persists its generation `system_fingerprint`, so a cross-fingerprint comparison like layer four's can't silently masquerade as a regression again. And the deploy gap — the sharpest of the six, where the repo's keepalive only pings `/health` and never exercises a route, so another missing `COPY` could ship green exactly as this one did — is now closed by a standing guard: a route-level [post-deploy wire-smoke](../.github/workflows/post-deploy-wire-smoke.yml) that runs after every deploy and fails on a bad `/ask/agent`. I trust that guard for a specific reason — I verified it the only way worth trusting, by **making it fail on purpose** — and that exercise immediately earned its keep: the first deliberate-failure run went red *for the wrong reason*, a subshell's non-zero exit tripping `bash -e` and killing the script before it could assert the branch it was built to test, so the run was red without ever testing what it claimed to. I fixed the capture and re-ran until both intolerant paths provably fail the job. The one instrument still caught only by my remembering is the corpus-scale delimiter check that flagged the Airgas 1-of-16 — a pass I ran by hand, not a standing test; the companion guard I'd still add is a chunk-size / multi-record check at ingestion, so a fat-chunk regression trips a gate instead of hiding behind a rising score.

Step back and the shape of the whole thing is a single climb. One lesson — *the metric triages; the artifact arbitrates* — carried up six layers of the stack, from the chunk that had to be findable to the deploy that had to actually contain the code. At the chunk layer a rank in the context window wasn't proof the model had used it (the rank probe had already over-reported outright, a piece before); at the answer layer the correctness delta didn't tell me the model used both values; at the threshold layer my own gate failed for the wrong reason; at the aggregate layer fingerprint drift impersonated a regression; at the hypothesis layer a sensible guess pointed at the wrong lever; at the deploy layer every test passed on code the artifact didn't contain. Every one was an instrument handing me a verdict the raw artifact had to overrule or complete, and every time the thing that told the truth was the artifact underneath it — the retrieved chunk, the generated answer, the matched-fingerprint read, the rank list, the raw wire response. The next piece — [how not to fool yourself](article-c-how-not-to-fool-yourself.md) — steps back from the individual bugs to the disciplines themselves — pre-registration, cheap falsification, and reading the artifact — as a method you can run on purpose.
